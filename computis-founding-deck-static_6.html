The problem, sized

The user problem

CPAs were spending 4–8 hours per crypto client on manual transaction classification — much of it on transactions that followed predictable patterns. Anomalies (high-impact mistakes) hid inside the volume. The audit trail lived in a spreadsheet.

The business problem

Computis was selling against incumbents (TaxBit, Cryptio, CoinTracker) whose AI features were treated as "black boxes" by the CPA market — the #1 G2 complaint across all three (42% of negative reviews).

Automation alone didn't differentiate. Several competitors had it.

The wedge hypothesis

If automation alone wasn't winning the market, the wedge was somewhere else — in how the AI behaved, not in what it did.

Three findings that broke our v1

Finding 1

CPAs reject opacity, not AI.

Of 12 CPAs interviewed, none objected to AI involvement in classification. 11 of 12 objected to the AI not showing its work. "The AI part is fine. The black box part is not."

Source: Discovery interviews · transcripts in research log

Finding 2

Audit defensibility > speed.

When asked to rank features, CPAs preferred "explanation per classification" over "faster classification" by ~3:1. The phrasing they used most: "How do I defend this to the IRS?"

Source: Card-sort exercise (R2) · n = 12

Finding 3

73% predictability is the wedge, not automation.

Transaction analysis: 73% of activity follows predictable patterns the AI handled at >90% confidence. The other 27% is where CPAs earn their fees — and where they need to feel in control.

Source: 50K-transaction corpus · ML-team confidence distribution

The combined signal: automate the 73% transparently, hand the 27% back with explanation, never hide the AI. This was the brief that killed v1.

The v1 → v2 pivot

v1 · REJECTED

"Hide the AI, show the answer."

Fully-automated classification. AI runs in the background; CPA sees a final result with a small "verified" badge. Override possible but buried.

Tested with 6 CPAs in week 7. 0 of 6 said they'd use it as their primary workflow. Verbatim concerns: "I'm not doing my job" · "How do I defend this?"

v2 · ADOPTED

"Show the AI's confidence. Hand back judgment."

Graduated autonomy. AI classifies with a confidence tier. High-confidence auto-applies; medium routes to a review queue; low requires manual judgment. Every classification has a rationale.

Tested with the same 6 CPAs in week 11. 6 of 6 said this matched their mental model.

Production "Design Evolution" artifact · v1 / v2 with measured outcomes

How I got the team there

The disagreement (week 7)

"Showing uncertainty is product damage."— CTO
"Hiding uncertainty is the actual product damage."— Me

What changed it

A 90-min joint session: CTO + lead CPA + me. The CTO watched a 15-year-career CPA push back and say "I would never use this."

The reframe — from the CTO:

"Overrides aren't corrections. They're additional context the AI doesn't have."

PARTNERSHIP PLAYBOOK · 3 MOVES

  1. Use the user as the third party. Designer–engineer disagreements become alignments when the user is in the room.
  2. Let the other side name the reframe. The CTO's line landed harder coming from him.
  3. Document the language. The reframe entered the spec the next day. Became how the team talked.
SECTION 03 · PIVOT
SLIDE 16 / 55[CORE]
[CORE] · 17 · PRINCIPLES → DECISIONS MAP

Three principles. Every decision traces back.

P1 · TRUST-FIRST

Admit uncertainty.

From: 11/12 CPAs reject opacity · v1 rejection in week 7

The position: the AI must say what it doesn't know. Confidence is a first-class UI element, not a hidden score.

Decisions traced to P1

  • 3-tier confidence badges on every classification
  • Override always 1-click reachable
  • Low-confidence transactions routed to manual queue (not auto-resolved)
  • "Why this confidence" disclosure on every badge

Trade-off accepted

Slower automation surface. v1 felt faster; v2 feels more honest. We chose honest.

P2 · TRANSPARENCY

Every AI decision has a "why."

From: 3:1 preference for explanation over speed · "How do I defend this?"

The position: rationale isn't a buried log — it's surfaced in-context, in plain language, on every screen where AI acted.

Decisions traced to P2

  • Plain-language rationale on every classification
  • Inline audit trail drawer (not export-only)
  • Anomaly explanations with severity + cause
  • Rule Builder shows NL preview ("If wallet matches X, classify as Y")

Trade-off accepted

More UI density per row. We chose density-with-context over sparse-but-mysterious.

P3 · AUGMENTATION

AI suggests. CPA decides.

From: professional liability framing · 73% predictable · 27% judgment

The position: the product earns its place by removing the predictable, not by replacing the judgment.

Decisions traced to P3

  • Override loop captures rationale → ML retraining signal
  • Rule Builder = CPAs encode logic without engineering tickets
  • Confidence threshold controls (firm-level)
  • Manual tier preserved (15% of volume) — by design, not by failure

Trade-off accepted

We did not chase 100% automation. The 15% manual tier is where CPAs justify their fees.

The solution — 3-tier AI confidence system

P1 · TRUST-FIRST

Confidence is a first-class UI element. The tier determines the workflow — not the other way around.

P2 · TRANSPARENCY

Every classification carries its tier + rationale. CPAs see exactly why the AI placed each transaction in each tier.

P3 · AUGMENTATION

High auto-classifies. Medium asks. Low defers entirely. Graduated autonomy — not all-or-nothing.

Information architecture

Top-level model

Workspace (firm)
  ├── Clients (per CPA)
  │   ├── Sources · wallets, exchanges, CSVs
  │   ├── Transactions · classified, queue, manual
  │   ├── Anomalies · flagged, resolved
  │   ├── Audit Trail · per-decision log
  │   └── Exports · 8949, summary, custom
  ├── Rules · firm-level + per-client
  └── Settings · thresholds, integrations

Choices made

  • Transactions over Reports as the primary surface — CPAs work bottom-up
  • Audit Trail as a peer-level node, not buried in Exports
  • Rules elevated to workspace-level — they're cross-client assets

IA choice that mattered most

The first IA draft put Audit Trail under Exports — treating it as a downstream artifact. Research synthesis flipped it: CPAs build defensibility as they classify, not after.

Surfacing the audit drawer inline (next slide) was the operationalization of that IA decision. Without the IA flip, the drawer would have been a downstream report nobody opens.

P2 · TRANSPARENCY

IA traces back: defensibility lives where the work happens, not in an export folder.

Token foundation

TYPE

Noto Sans + JetBrains Mono for numerics and rules. Five-step ramp (H1–H3 + Body + Small) with explicit weight + LH per step. Numerics in mono for column scan-ability.

COLOR

Three principle accents (P1/P2/P3) over a 9-step neutral. Three confidence tiers (green/amber/red) — semantic, never decorative. WCAG AA on every combination.

SPACING

8-px grid · 6-step ramp · spacing tokens named by intent (gap-row, pad-card) not size, so semantic refactors don't break layouts.

Choice points

Why Noto Sans? Multilingual support (firms with international clients), high x-height for dense table reading, neutral character that doesn't compete with the data.

Why JetBrains Mono for numerics? Tabular figures keep columns aligned without explicit font-feature-settings. CPAs scan columns of money and addresses; alignment is legibility.

What this replaced. The codebase shipped with a Bootstrap default stack (mixed serif/sans on tables). I unified it in week 3 — first system change.

Governance rule

Tier colors cannot be used as decorative accents anywhere in the product. Component lint checks for --tier-* usage outside <ConfidenceBadge> and <Anomaly>. This kept "amber" from drifting into "warning toast" territory and diluting the signal.

Transactions — the daily-driver surface

Transactions list with inline confidence badges, AI rationale on hover, single-click override

What to look at

01 Confidence badge inline. Tier color + text label. Color alone never carries meaning.

02 Mono numerics. Money and addresses align without setting tabular-nums per cell.

03 Density target: 14 rows visible. CPA workflow demands above-fold scan; sub-14 felt sparse, 17+ sacrificed legibility.

04 Override is a row action, not a modal. The drawer comes for context — the override itself is one click.

P1 · TRUST-FIRST

Density and trust pull against each other. The badge anchors trust; the row layout earns the density.

Audit drawer — the trust surface

Drawer, not modal

CPAs review in sequences. Modals interrupt; drawers preserve row context.

Contents · priority order

  1. Plain-language rationale — one sentence
  2. Source rule(s) — fired in what order
  3. Confidence breakdown — what pushed the score
  4. Similar past decisions — consistency check
  5. Override + reason — captures training signal

P2 · TRANSPARENCY

"How do I defend this to the IRS?" → every classification ships with a paper trail.

EDGE CASE LOG

  • Unknown rule. Sub-50% confidence + no rule → "Manual review required" + 3-question diagnostic. Not an empty state.
  • Conflicting rules. Drawer shows precedence, lets CPA promote a rule inline.
  • Stale data. FMV >6h old → banner + one-click refresh.

Override loop — the AI's training signal

The mechanic

Every override captures:

  1. Original AI suggestion
  2. CPA's correction
  3. One-line reason · fixed taxonomy of 8

Taxonomy hand-built with 4 of 12 interview CPAs.

The design tension

Override = highest-friction surface. Reason input had to be:

  • Fast enough not to deter overrides (else signal lost)
  • Structured enough to be machine-readable (else signal = noise)

3 iterations: free-text → free-text + tags → fixed taxonomy + optional note. Final cut input time 2.4×.

SLIDE 31 / 55[CUTTABLE]
[CORE · DEEP-DIVE] · 32

Smart wallet upload — the on-ramp problem

Smart wallet upload — drag-drop CSV with auto-detection of source exchange and inline column mapping

The problem

CPAs onboard with files from 15+ exchanges, each with a different CSV schema. The original onboarding required choosing the source from a dropdown of 40+ options. Drop-off at this step was 38%.

The redesign

Auto-detection. Drop the file; the parser sniffs the columns and proposes a mapping. CPA confirms or corrects.

Inline column mapping. Wrong column? Drag-and-drop on the table header instead of a separate mapping screen.

Error context. Bad rows quarantine instead of failing the upload. CPA can fix or skip them.

Outcome

CPA onboarding time ↓45%. The single biggest contributor to the verified onboarding metric. [VERIFIED]

P3 · AUGMENTATION

Don't replace the CPA's judgment about what file came from where — accelerate it. The CPA still confirms; the system just removes the busywork.

How design made the AI legible.

The AI is the engine. Design is the dashboard. Without the dashboard, no CPA touches the engine.
SLIDE 33 / 55

Three trust surfaces, one model

SURFACE 1 · BADGE

Where: every transaction row, every classification.

What it carries: tier (HIGH/MED/LOW) + a numeric score on hover.

What it doesn't: the rationale. Click for the drawer.

Trust at-a-glance.

SURFACE 2 · DRAWER

Where: click any row.

What it carries: rationale, source rules, similar past decisions, override.

What it doesn't: bulk operations. The drawer is single-decision.

Trust on demand.

SURFACE 3 · EXPORTS

Where: audit-ready exports for IRS / firm review.

What it carries: per-line rationale + rule citations + confidence score.

What it doesn't: live overrides — the export is a snapshot.

Trust as an artifact.

The principle behind it

Trust isn't a feature. It's a layered design problem.

  • "Which decisions need my attention?" → badge
  • "Why did the AI decide this?" → drawer
  • "How do I defend this to a third party?" → exports

No surface tries to do all three.

P1 · TRUST-FIRST

89% High-tier acceptance ≠ "the AI got better." It's "CPAs trust the High signal." The badge has to be earned at the model level and be readable at the design level. Both, or neither works.

Miss #1 — the manual review queue exceeded projection

MISS   What I projected

Pivot doc: Low-tier would settle at 5–7%. Defended the team-size assumption.

MISS   What happened

Low tier ran at 11% through 6 weeks of pilot.

One of four pilot firms hit Low-tier saturation by Wednesday. Forced staffing-assumption conversations during sales.

LEARN   Why I missed it

Projection used hand-classified CPA data. That data overrepresented confident classifications because CPAs hand-skip ambiguous transactions.

The model can't skip. It has to score everything.

LEARN   What changed

  1. Defer-to-end-of-batch flag — emulates hand-skip
  2. Sales now names 11% directly, not 7%

P3 · AUGMENTATION

Augmentation projections fail when they assume AI mirrors human behavior. Humans skip hard cases; AI can't. The system has to expose the asymmetry, not paper over it.

Attribution — separating design from confounds

The honest answer

The 32% demo-to-customer conversion lift Q3–Q4 vs. Q1–Q2 is partially attributable to design — not entirely. Two known confounds:

(1) Sales team training change (rolled out wk 18) — coincident with the bigger UI changes shipping.

Procurement teams cited transparency / audit defensibility in 78% of recorded calls as the differentiating reason. Transparency is a design property — the audit drawer, the per-line rationale in exports, the confidence-tier disclosure. That's the cleanest signal.

[BACKUP · B6] · VISUAL RULE BUILDER DEEP-DIVE
B6 · BACKUP — "Tell me about the Visual Rule Builder."

Visual Rule Builder — no-code rule authoring

Drag-drop rule clauses · natural-language preview · live test against last 30-day transactions

Why it earned its slide

Single biggest contributor to ↓85% engineering dependency. Pre-Builder: every rule = code change. Post-Builder: CPA-authored, version-controlled.

Three design choices

  1. Natural-language preview. Every clause renders as English. Lowers cognitive load of nested logic.
  2. Live test, last 30 days. Pre-save, CPA sees what the rule would have classified. Catches over-broad rules.
  3. Component-driven. Composed from <RuleClause>. Shipped in 6 weeks because components existed.

P3 · AUGMENTATION

CPAs as rule authors didn't reduce engineering — it freed engineering for model + infrastructure work.
